How we test Doorman

No scam checker catches everything, and anyone who says otherwise isn't being straight with you. Here's how we measure Doorman, and what it misses.

What we test against

We use large collections of real examples that researchers and security groups publish, so our results can be checked by anyone with the same data.

  • Live phishing links: 789,052 links from Phishing.Database, a public list of active phishing sites (MIT licence).
  • Genuine websites: the 1,000,000 most-visited websites from Google's Chrome UX Report (CC BY 4.0).
  • Fake look-alike addresses: 448,226 addresses we generated from the 141 brands Doorman protects, using the same method security teams use to find look-alike domains (misspellings, swapped letters, letters from other alphabets and so on).
  • Real scam texts: 23,899 scam messages reported by the public, collected by researchers at University College London (CC BY 4.0).
  • Genuine texts and emails: the UCI SMS collection and the SpamAssassin email collection, both widely used in research.
  • Disguised messages: every scam and genuine text again, rewritten six ways scammers hide words from filters, such as invisible characters and letters from other alphabets.

We set half of the real scam texts aside and never look at them while writing new rules. Results on that unseen half tell us whether Doorman works on scams it has never seen.

What counts as a warning

Doorman gives one of three results: likely scam, caution (some warning signs), or no common warning signs. In the results below, "a warning" means likely scam or caution.

What Doorman misses, and why

  • "Wrong number" openers. A first message like "Hi, are we still on for Friday?" looks exactly like a genuine text. The scam only shows itself later in the conversation.
  • Brand-new scam sites with ordinary-looking names. If a site's address and page look normal, there may be nothing for Doorman's checks to notice. That's why Pro adds a daily list of reported scam sites.
  • Links removed from research data. Many of the real scam texts we test with had their links removed to protect privacy, so Doorman can only judge the words. In real messages it checks the link too.

So treat "no common warning signs" as exactly that, not as proof something is safe.

How we keep false alarms down

Every new rule is tested against the genuine collections before it's added. If it starts flagging genuine messages or popular websites, it's changed or dropped. Doorman also knows the 100,000 most-visited websites, so small oddities in their addresses don't cause false alarms.

Spotted a mistake?

If Doorman missed a scam or warned about something genuine, tell us at hello@survivelabs.co.uk. Please don't send us personal details: a description or a version with names removed is plenty.
