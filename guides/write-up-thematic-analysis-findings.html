How to write up thematic analysis findings in your dissertation

You've coded your interviews and found your themes. Now you need to turn them into a findings chapter that reads well and convinces your examiner. Here's a structure that works, and how to use quotes properly.

What your findings chapter is for

Your findings (or results) chapter tells the story your data tells about your research question. It isn't a list of everything participants said. It presents your themes, shows the evidence for each one, and explains what each theme means.

Some departments want findings and discussion as separate chapters; others prefer them combined. Check your handbook. If they're separate, the findings chapter says what you found and the discussion chapter links it to the literature.

A structure that works

1

A short introduction with an overview

In a paragraph, remind the reader of your research question and say how many themes you developed. Then show them all at once in a table or a simple diagram (often called a thematic map), so the reader knows where the chapter is going.

ThemeSub-themesInterviews it appeared in
Losing trust in banksA bad experience; Feeling unheard8 of 10
Family as a safety netBorrowing from relatives; Family arguments7 of 10
Staying in controlAvoiding apps; Paper budgets5 of 10

An example overview table from a made-up study about how people manage money.

2

One section per theme

Give each theme its own heading and follow the same pattern each time:

  • Name and define it. One or two sentences saying what the theme is about.
  • Explain it. Your interpretation: what participants were getting at, and how it answers your question.
  • Show the evidence. Two to four well-chosen quotes, each followed by your comment on it.
  • Note differences. Did some participants see it differently? Saying so makes your analysis more convincing, not less.
3

A short summary

End the chapter with a paragraph pulling the themes together and leading into your discussion.

How to use quotes well

  • Analyse, don't just quote. Every quote needs at least a sentence from you saying why it matters. A run of quotes with no comment reads as a summary, not an analysis.
  • Keep most quotes short. A sentence or two is usually enough. Use longer quotes, set apart as a block, only when the detail matters.
  • Use pseudonyms or codes. Label each quote with a made-up name or a code like P3, and never include details that could identify someone.
  • Trim carefully. Use an ellipsis (...) to show you've cut words, and square brackets to add a word for clarity, such as "[the bank] froze my account". Never change the meaning.
  • Spread your quotes around. If every quote comes from the same two people, the reader will wonder about everyone else.

A useful check: cover the quotes with your hand and read only your own sentences. If the argument still makes sense, you've analysed the data. If it falls apart, you've let the quotes do the work.

Should you use numbers?

Saying a theme came up in "8 of 10 interviews" can help the reader see how common it was, and a table like the one above is a neat way to show it. But a theme doesn't matter more just because more people mentioned it. If you use counts, use them lightly, and keep the focus on meaning.

Vague words like "many" or "some" can be just as misleading as numbers. If you use them, be consistent.

Common mistakes

  • Themes named after your interview questions. A theme called "Experiences of banking" just describes a topic. A theme like "Losing trust in banks" makes a point.
  • Too many themes. Three to six themes is typical for a dissertation. Twelve themes means you probably haven't finished grouping.
  • Bringing in the literature too early. If your department wants separate chapters, save comparisons with other studies for the discussion.
  • Forgetting your question. Each theme should help answer it. If one doesn't, it may belong in an appendix or not at all.

Before you hand it in

  • Does the introduction show all your themes at a glance?
  • Does every theme have a clear name, a definition and your interpretation?
  • Is every quote followed by your comment?
  • Are all participants anonymised?
  • Does each theme link back to your research question?

Questions people ask

How many quotes should I use per theme?

There's no fixed rule, but two to four well-chosen quotes per theme is common in a dissertation, each followed by your own explanation. Pick quotes that show the theme clearly and come from a range of participants.

Should findings and discussion be separate?

It depends on your department and your approach. Some prefer separate chapters, others a combined “findings and discussion” chapter. Check your module handbook and ask your supervisor.

How long should a findings chapter be?

It varies by course, but the findings chapter is often one of the longest in a qualitative dissertation. Check your handbook for word guidance and divide the words roughly evenly between your themes.

Can I include a thematic map?

Yes. A simple diagram or table showing your themes and sub-themes helps the reader see the whole picture before the detail. Keep it clear and readable.
