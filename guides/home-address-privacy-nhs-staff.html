Is your home address online? A privacy guide for NHS staff

Your name is on your badge, your door, your letters and sometimes the ward board. Most patients and families are grateful. A few aren't, and they can search your name on the bus home.

Updated 18 November 2026 · 5 minute read

On this page
  1. Why it matters
  2. A 10-minute check
  3. What can be removed
  4. Questions

Why healthcare staff should check

Doctors, nurses, paramedics, midwives and therapists share their names all day as part of good care. That name, plus your hospital or surgery and a profile photo, is often enough for free websites to suggest where you live and who with.

Healthcare means dealing with people on their worst days. Grief, fear and anger sometimes land on whoever was in the room. Some patients become fixated on a member of staff. Your employer can deal with what happens at work. What's online about your home is something you can deal with yourself.

It matters even more if you live near where you work, which many people in smaller towns do.

A 10-minute check you can do tonight

You don't need any special tools. Open a private browser window, so your search history doesn't change the results, and try these:

  1. Your full name in quotes, plus your town. For example "Jane Smith" Cardiff. Look past the first page of results.
  2. Your name plus your street name. If anything comes up, your address is linked to your name somewhere.
  3. Your mobile number and personal email address. These often lead back to old listings, sign-ups and profiles.
  4. Your name plus the people you live with. Some websites list "possible relatives" and "people at this address".
  5. Your profile photo, using a reverse image search. This shows where else your face appears.

Be careful with "remove my data" buttons on websites you don't recognise. Some ask for more personal details, or payment, before they do anything. Write down what you find first, then decide what to do.

What can be removed, and what can't

UK data protection law gives you the right to ask a company to delete your personal data. Companies normally have one month to reply, and many simply comply. You can also give someone written permission to make these requests for you.

Some things can't be removed, because the law says they must stay public. Property sale prices are one example. The aim is not to vanish from the internet. It's to break the easy chain from your name, to your home, to the people you live with.

Order matters too. Some websites copy each other, so a detail you remove from one can come straight back from another. Doing the removals in the right order, and checking again a few weeks later, is what makes them stick.
